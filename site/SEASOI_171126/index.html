Agent-based modeling

We have established why cities are difficult to delimit and why their climate relationships cross scales. I now turn from that urban framing to the basic ideas of agent-based modeling. The aim is to make the entities, assumptions and sources of collective behavior understandable before discussing software. Transition: We can locate ABM at the intersection of several intellectual traditions.

Three intellectual roots

Artificial intelligence: An intelligent entity versus Distributed-AI where intelligence comes from the collective.

Multi-agent systems: interacting software or robotic agents in a shared environment.

Complex systems: cross-disciplinary study of collective dynamics, such as flocking.

[Reynolds, 1987] | Images: Easy-Peasy.AI (AI-generated) via www.vpnsrus.com (CC BY 2.0) · RoboCup, Inria · Walter Baxter (CC BY-SA 2.0)
Artificial intelligence can focus on the behavior of one entity, whereas distributed approaches ask how capacities may arise through a collective. Multi-agent systems study interacting real or virtual agents and connect software engineering with fields such as robotics. Complex-systems research crosses biology, mathematics, computing and other disciplines to study patterns such as flocking. Crowd animation, traffic, ecology, epidemiology and land-use modeling all make use of individually represented entities. Their decisions and interactions can be inspected or redesigned separately rather than encoded only as a global aggregate equation. A collective pattern is emergent in the modeling sense when that pattern is generated through local mechanisms rather than directly imposed as the outcome. This does not remove global constraints, environmental processes or modeler choices from the simulation. Transition: Let us bring these examples together around their shared generative idea.

Different examples, one generative idea

The idea: Autonomous local entities + interactions → collective outcomes

Food web: KeyStageWiki, CC BY-NC 4.0 · Herd immunity: Tkarcher, CC BY-SA 4.0
These examples share the idea of representing local entities and their interactions, even though their purposes and levels of detail differ. The collective outcome is generated through those mechanisms rather than prescribed as a global result. This common principle does not mean that all applications need the same assumptions or complexity. Transition: The appropriate amount of detail depends on what the model is useful for.

From the idea to the agent-based model

An ABM is a conceptual object that symbolically represents a system, encoded in a computer program to simulate and to explore in silico the consequences of its constitutive hypotheses.

Narratives, diagrams and rules organize the hypotheses.

Texts, images and pseudocode make the representation explicit.

A codebase, APIs and software implement those hypotheses.

A computer unfolds the model's dynamics through time.

Runs compare parameter values, assumptions and alternative versions.

Agent decisions making and interactions can give rise to meso- and macro-level outcomes.

What exactly do we do when we design ABMs?

Icons: Lucide contributors · ISC license
We begin with a conceptual account of a target system and express its hypotheses symbolically. Encoding and simulation turn that account into an executable experiment, not into a literal copy of reality. Exploration compares what follows from different assumptions and configurations, especially when local mechanisms generate larger-scale outcomes. Transition: To understand what we actually build, let us separate three components.

George Box reminded statisticians that every model simplifies reality and is therefore wrong in some respect. The real question is whether a given simplification is useful for a stated purpose. KISS · Understand

[Edmonds & Moss, 2005] · [Ferguson et al., 2020, Report 9]
KILT · Social learning
A model can be a transitive learning instrument:
Lower-Amazon fisheries agreements · Fishbanks.
[Le Page & Perrotton, 2018, KILT] · [MIT Sloan, Fishbanks]
I am adapting Box's aphorism here; the inserted phrase agent-based is not part of his original wording. KISS emphasizes explanatory simplicity, while KIDS argues for descriptive adequacy when the problem demands it, and these are orientations rather than a strict taxonomy. A bounded-confidence model can produce polarization without establishing that all extremism has the same mechanism. Fishbanks illustrates learning through simulation; the lower-Amazon fisheries and Lubrizol or ESCAPE cases are workshop examples, not quantified findings claimed here. Transition: Across these purposes, what is the activity we call agent-based modeling?

An ABM in a nutshell

  1. Agents
  2. Environment
  3. Interactions, relationships
    and feedback
Illustration taken from [Ramadiah & Galbiati, 2021]
Agents, environment and interactions are three complementary ways to describe the same model. The environment provides context and resources, while agents represent the entities with relevant autonomy. Interactions include relationships and feedback, not only isolated actions. Transition: First, who can count as an agent?

Who are the agents?

An autonomous entity / a piece of software / a set of algorithms that...

Perceive variables / attributes in a range, filtered or biased, searched for or received - often multiple sensors
Decide and plan from purely reactive to complex cognitive processes
Act and interact change or distort the state of other agents and the environment
Optionally learn and adapt Utility function, simple internal update or complex memory based evolution
[Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995] · [Gilbert, 2007]
An agent is an autonomous entity, a piece of software or a set of algorithms that perceives its environment, decides and acts. Decision can range from purely reactive rules, like foraging ants, to cognitive architectures such as BEN with beliefs, emotions and norms, and language models now offer yet another way to generate action. Learning and adaptation are optional, and Tay is a reminder of the risk: Microsoft took its Twitter chatbot offline within a day after a coordinated attack exploited a vulnerability in what it learned from users. Whatever the engine, we still need to explain its assumptions and assess whether it suits the purpose. Transition: Now, which entities can play the role of an agent?

Who are the agents?

Not only human individuals but...

Groups · animals · firms
Nations · cities · any other active entities
Agnostic by design

Equations · rules · pseudocode
Graphical models · LLM-powered decisions

Occam's razor, complexity / complications and the integronster problem [Voinov & Shugart, 2013]

[Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995]
An agent's referent can be an individual or a collective entity, such as a firm, an animal group or a city. The internal mechanism is not prescribed: equations, symbolic rules, graphical formalisms or a language-model component can all be candidates. More complexity is not automatically more validity, so the engine should follow the question. Transition: Agents also require an environment, which need not resemble a geographical map.

Regular grid, torus and other non regular spaces

Classic smallworld, scalefree, but also spatially explicit, dynamic or uniform

"The notion of an agent is meant to be a tool for analyzing systems, not an absolute characterization that divides the world into agents and non-agents." [Russell et al., 1995]

[GAMA documentation, model]
An environment can be a realistic territory, an abstract grid or a set of social relations without explicit geography. It can change through time, provide shared resources or simply constrain possible actions. Representing the environment as an agent is a software and modeling choice, not a claim that everything in the real world has human-like intentions. GAMA makes the model itself an agent, illustrating how flexible this boundary can become. Transition: What matters next is what agents do to one another and to those shared environments.

What do agents do?

Communicate and act

Give · take · move · extract
share · switch · use…

Actions may affect the inner state of agents and their environment

Affect others

Directly by e.g. sharing information
Indirectly through e.g. fishing from a shared stock

Local actions feed collective outcomes through space and time.

Generate feedback loops

A rumor can prompt withdrawals
withdrawals can help cause the feared failure

The inequality spiral is a mechanims, not the explanation

[Epstein & Axtell, 1996] · [Gardner, 1970] · [Coleman, 1986]
Agent actions include communication, but also ordinary changes to locations, resources and other agents: Sugarscape illustrates resource use and movement, while the Game of Life shows how local state changes generate evolving patterns. An action can change someone else's conditions directly, through a message, or indirectly, through a shared stock, as illustrated here by ecoParc, developed with students at the MISSABMS summer school. The bank-run example shows a possible positive feedback loop in which a belief changes behavior and behavior helps create the believed outcome. The Coleman boat, shown here in an illustration credited to Gianluca Manzo, connects social conditions, individual actions and their aggregate consequences. Transition: This leads to two central principles of the agent-based perspective.

Microfoundations and generativity

Microfoundations
Agents are the smallest autonomous active entities at the chosen resolution.

Generativity
Collective patterns arise through local actions and interactions—not a prescribed global outcome.

[Epstein & Axtell, 1996]
Micro does not necessarily mean a person: it means the smallest autonomous active entity at the resolution chosen for this model. Generativity concerns producing a collective pattern through the implemented mechanisms rather than inserting the target pattern as an output rule. That does not mean an ABM has no aggregate equations or constraints; it means that the phenomenon we call emergent is not simply prescribed. Transition: We can experience that distinction by becoming the agents ourselves.

Heroes & Cowards—you are the agents

Secretly choose two people: a friend and an enemy

Hero: stay between your friend and your enemy
Coward: keep your friend between you and your enemy

Can you guess what will be the collective pattern?

[Stonedahl, Wilensky & Rand, 2014]
Each of us chooses a friend and an enemy without announcing those choices. A hero tries to protect the friend by standing between the two, whereas a coward tries to hide behind the friend. We move slowly with room around us, observing what collective pattern follows without anyone directing that pattern. Transition: We can now connect this generative idea to three urban applications.

Agent-based modeling in risk and crisis management

Risk and crisis management

IPCC risk framework · ANR-24-CE39-5449 ATEsT
Risk is not only the physical hazard; it also depends on who and what is exposed, and on vulnerability. The IPCC framework helps connect these dimensions rather than treating them as separate problems. Crisis research, response systems and coordination therefore need to be considered together. Transition: These interactions appear in very different real-world crises.

Four crises, four reasons to model interaction

Hurricane Rita · evacuation
107 of 113 Texas deaths were indirect; many related to evacuation activities. Carpender et al. (2006)
Indian Ocean tsunami · warning
About 230,000 deaths; recognizing natural warning signs can cue immediate evacuation. UNESCO · US NWS
Flash floods · daily mobility
Over half of flood drownings involve vehicles driven into floodwater. US NWS
Cyclone Chido · response capacity
Damaged infrastructure and inaccessible areas complicated response and casualty counts. AP/NPR (2024)
Rita is a caution against reducing evacuation harm to panic: the study classified most Texas deaths as indirect, with many associated with evacuation activities. The Indian Ocean tsunami killed about 230,000 people; recognizing natural warning signs can prompt movement before an official alert, though warning systems and accessible routes remain essential. US flood-safety guidance shows how routine driving can intersect with dangerous water. Chido also highlights how infrastructure damage and inaccessible places make response and casualty accounting difficult. Transition: These are different pathways from risk to action, and each requires careful modelling assumptions.

Two places, different evacuation questions

HANOI · FLOOD AND MIXED TRAFFIC

Wetland city; recurring floods and informal readiness. The Hoa Binh co-model was limited by poor street-level elevation data.

Phuc Xa scenario: 3 exit road widening -> 15% evacuation time gained

Pareto front of staged evacuation strategies for mean evac time (2.12 ratio) / time spent on road (2.1 ratio)

GUADELOUPE · SOUFRIÈRE VOLCANO

Explore evacuation pathways and awareness around volcanic risk.

Scenario exploration, not a forecast of volcanic behavior.

Chapuis et al. (2021) · Chapuis et al. (2022) · Daudé et al. (2019)
Hanoi is built on a dried wetland, and urban artificialization increases street impermeability; recurring floods leave residents reliant on informal preparedness, with limited authority-led plans. Along the Red River, informal settlements border a dike road connecting the airport and downtown; it had been rebuilt the year before the study discussion. The Hoa Binh dam-break co-model could not support central-city inundation analysis because its elevation data lacked street-level detail, so the work turned to Phuc Xa mixed-traffic scenarios. One modeled road-widening scenario reduced evacuation time from 1 hour 18 minutes to 1 hour 2 minutes; Guadeloupe's Soufrière scenarios also supported evacuation awareness, but neither result is an observed intervention effect. Transition: Together, the cases show the value and limits of combining mobility, hazard and spatial evidence.

TsunaWAVE from tsunami alert system to evacuation

Hazard · Alert systems · Population response

Carles et al. (2024) · TsunaWAVE
TsunaWAVE links tsunami hazard scenarios to warning systems and population response. The French warning chain runs from institutional alerts to sirens or cell broadcast and then to people deciding what to do. Evacuation also depends on local access to beaches, slopes and shelters. It's what's inside that counts

Agents

Dummies: milling time is probabilist, they do not gather or organize themselves, and interact only when they cannot find a route

Environment

Roads · ZTEs · slopes · warning (noise modeling) · hazard scenario

Interactions

Test alert timing reliability and response assumptions

Routes, access, slopes and safe places define evacuation timing

Carles et al. (2024) · TsunaWAVE · VR prototype
The model represents individuals with a sampled milling time after an alert; people may search for information, confirm the warning and prepare, but agents do not normally gather or communicate. They interact only when unable to find a route, while scenarios combine roads, ZTEs, slopes, warning and hazard with examples from Cannes, Sanary and Mamoudzou. The model compares alert channels and failures under alternative response assumptions; TsunaPRIME and TsunaWAVE document and explore outcomes, while VR is intended for awareness. These are modeled tools and scenarios, not evidence that an intervention has been evaluated. Transition: The main contribution is to expose how warning and context affect modeled evacuation, with clear limits.

ATEsT: improved civil security doctrine for a population inclusive crisis management

Citizen participation · Emergent risks · Situational awarness

Sildaro illustrations, project partner · ATEsT project
ATEsT addresses how crisis management can adapt to emerging risks and involve inhabitants during early response. It has two connected directions: a sandbox grounded in operational doctrine and agent-based simulation, and companion modelling to explore cooperation. The images show the available GAMA and Sildaro materials; the R-io visual referenced in the brief was not present among the local assets. These are project aims, not demonstrated outcomes. Transition: The three working ideas are doctrine, a sandbox for exploring consequences and cooperation across roles.

Approche Transdisciplinaire des risques Émergents sur un Territoire

Emergent risks required a revised doctrine and more inclusive crisis management

Coupling MDE · MBKR · ABM foster by companion modeling approach

GAMA · R-io
The first route is to express business doctrine in an ontology so that participants can inspect its assumptions. The second uses an agent-based sandbox to explore how actions may produce consequences that are not obvious in advance. The third uses companion modelling to develop institutional and non-institutional cooperation. Inhabitants can be at risk, passive, self-protecting, helping or organized through groups such as VISOV; professionals include municipal staff, health workers, police, firefighters and sometimes military personnel. Transition: The takeaway is a shared learning process, not an automated crisis authority.
